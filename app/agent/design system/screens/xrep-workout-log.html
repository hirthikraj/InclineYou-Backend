Workout log

Fifteen states for the screen a trainer holds between sets, and a teardown of nine platforms that log a workout. Every one of them was built for the lifter logging for themselves — their own phone, their own numbers, their own signal. Train X is built for a trainer logging for someone else: a phone propped on a rack in a basement gym in Adyar, thirty seconds of quiet, a client waiting, and no bars. That changes what goes on the screen. The previous numbers stop being nice to have and become the point; a swap stops being a mistake and becomes data; and the network stops being allowed to have an opinion about whether a set happened.

01 · The floor screen

One exercise open, and last Monday already in the row

The log opens from a booked session and takes the whole phone. There is no tab bar, because mid-session there is nowhere else to be — this is a mode, not a tab. One exercise is expanded and the rest are rows, so the thing under your thumb is always the thing you are doing. The set table runs the full width of the screen and the Previous column is the reason it is allowed to: a trainer who can see what she lifted last Monday does not have to remember it, and that is the whole advantage this screen has over the notebook it replaces.

02 · A set

Four numbers, and the two nobody else asks for

Load and reps live in the row and are typed with the numeric keypad. RPE and a note are one long press away — they are the two things the trainer knows and the client's own app never records, and they are the reason a log written by a coach is worth more than a log written by a lifter. Everything here is a sheet rather than a screen, because you are holding a phone in one hand. And nothing on this screen asks “are you sure”: undo after, never confirm before.

03 · Off-plan

The rack was busy, and that is not an error

In an Indian gym the rack you planned for has somebody else in it, and the cable station has a queue. Every logger in the category treats a deviation as a failure: an exercise you skipped, a workout you did not complete, a red mark against adherence. Train X treats it as the highest-signal thing a trainer does all week. An unplanned exercise is a first-class row — same card, same table, counted in volume. And a swap is recorded against the program, not buried in the session, so next week's plan knows it happened.

04 · The record

Gold, and the threshold that keeps it worth something

A record is the only thing in a training log a client will read twice, so it is the one moment the app is allowed to be loud. Train X computes records when it reads the sets and never stores them, which is not an implementation note — it is the difference between a number you can trust and a number you have to reconcile. Correct a set you typed wrong three weeks ago and every record that depended on it fixes itself in the same frame. And because gold is cheap to hand out, there is a threshold: a first log is not a record, and matching is not beating.

A first log gets no gold at all: it is the number to beat, not a number beaten.

05 · Progress & PRs

Three figures, one chart, and no chart that flatters

Progress is what the trainer opens on the way to the gym and what they turn the phone round to show. It carries three things: volume, the top set, and bodyweight — and only one of them gets a chart. Volume is a sum, so bars from zero tell the truth about it. Load is not: a bar chart of 52.5 kg against 55 kg starting at zero makes a plate look like nothing, so the top set is written out as the sequence of numbers it actually is. Every figure on this screen also appears as words, because a trainer reads it out loud.

06 · When it goes wrong

No signal, no plan, and the one tap that costs a session

Three states, and only one of them is a failure. Anytime Fitness Adyar has its free-weights floor a level below the road, so the gym is where the signal is not — which is why the phone already holds the program, the last eight weeks of her sets and the whole exercise library. Offline, one banner appears and nothing else changes. There are no skeletons anywhere on this screen: local data paints instantly or the offline claim is a lie. The second state is the end of a session, where somebody has to decide what it cost. The third is a client with no program, which is the likeliest first screen a new trainer ever sees.

What everyone got right

The Previous column, and per-exercise rest. Hevy and Strong between them solved the mechanics of logging a set years ago, and there is no reason to redraw a solved table. Strava solved the record: derive it, never store it.

What everyone got wrong

Every one of the nine assumes the person logging is the person lifting. Consumer loggers optimise for your own numbers on your own phone; coaching platforms put logging behind a client-facing app and hand the trainer a review queue. Neither is what happens on a gym floor in Chennai, where one phone records four clients a morning.

What we deliberately skipped

No supersets in v1 — a superset is a UI for a lifter optimising their own hour, and a trainer supervising one client can simply log two exercises. No plate calculator screen, no 1RM chart, no social anything. And no auto-progression: the app never puts a number in a row that nobody lifted.

What India adds

Three things. Basement gyms — the free-weights floor is a level below the road and 4G does not reach it, so offline is the normal case and not the edge case. Shared racks — the rack you planned for has somebody in it, so a swap is a Tuesday, not an exception, and an app that scores it as non-adherence is wrong. And the trainer logs, not the client: the client's phone is a 32 GB Android with no room for another app and no interest in one. Plus a small thing that decides the maths: the smallest plate in the room is 1.25 kg in some gyms and 2.5 kg in others, so the step is per-gym.
